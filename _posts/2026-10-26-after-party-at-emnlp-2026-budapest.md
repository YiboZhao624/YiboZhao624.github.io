Glad yet exhausted after attending the Day 3 social event at EMNLP 2026 in Budapest. More and more researchers from diverse fields are gathering at this conference, enjoying the discussions, wine, and music, while carrying an unspoken anxiety about the future.

I hate to be a party pooper, but I need to get this off my chest: the DJ set at the social event felt like the final night of our traditional research journey—something everyone tacitly senses, even if no one wants to say it out loud.

Where does this anxiety come from? Perhaps it starts with how the nature of our work has changed.

Later that night, I talked about future plans with my labmate. He admitted that he had lost his interest in research because he was tired of the endless rat race. When the community rewards only the fastest rather than the deepest work, it leaves no room for human deep thinking—only for GPUs' "deep thinking." **Research begins to feel like an industrialized assembly line, and frankly, that is boring.**

Worse still, this speed race is breaking the foundation of our community. Everyone feels pressured to flood conferences with as many papers as possible before the peer-review system completely collapses. Pioneers spent decades building the reputation of these top venues, only for us to exhaust that credibility in just the last two years.

And when papers are mass-produced and lose their value as a reliable signal, people naturally turn to something else. During conversations with several accomplished researchers, I repeatedly heard the same phrase: papers matter less and less, and *"connection is all you need."* Technical discussions quickly turned into pure networking sessions—trading referrals and collecting contacts. **Watching academic currency hyperinflate and give way to social networking was deeply discouraging.**

To me, research—and perhaps life itself—is meant to be **an infinite game**. The rules evolve, players come and go, and the true goal is simply to keep the game in play. Yet we have turned it into a finite game of short-term extraction, crowded with more and more people simply chasing lucrative industry packages.

Still, this very climate makes me clearer about why I want to continue pursuing a PhD—**for intellectual freedom and for true discovery.** At its best, a PhD program may be the last sanctuary for idealists to do what they genuinely care about.

Instead of exploiting a dying game, I want to give myself more time to explore real, difficult problems in the world. It is urgently time to change the rules, and I still value the chance to keep the real game alive.
